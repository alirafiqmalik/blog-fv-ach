Credit Without Debit Formal Verification of Banking Payment Systems

Ali Hamza Malik
University of Massachusetts Amherst
Muhammad Taqi Raza
University of Massachusetts Amherst
TL;DR

We model U.S. ACH bank transfers as a multi-day, multi-bank protocol and model-check it against three core security invariants — all three break. The counterexamples reveal Credit-Without-Debit (CWD), a new attack class in which spendable money appears at one institution while the matching debit fails at another. We confirmed three instances at live U.S. banks, card issuers, and payment apps.

01 · The problem

A bank transfer is not one event

Settled ACH payments are permanent, so everyone assumes debits and credits always pair up and that pending money cannot be spent.

But one transfer is a multi-day pipeline across independent institutions: the ODFI authorizes it, the ACH Operator batches it, the RDFI applies access control, and settlement arrives 3–5 days later. The Nacha rules leave two choices open, and many institutions take the convenient option:

  • Authorization is a one-time check at submission — often only a valid routing number.
  • Provisional credit becomes spendable before the receiving bank agrees to pay.

The usual mental model treats the transfer as atomic. Toggle to see what it hides.

Threat model: no insider, no stolen password

The attacker uses only what an ordinary customer has:

C-1 · own accounts
Ordinary, KYC-verified bank accounts.
C-2 · public identifiers
Routing and account numbers from any check. No login credentials.
C-3 · consumer interfaces
Starts ACH transfers in apps, and picks amount and SEC code.
C-4 · account controls
Sets holds or debit blocks on their own accounts.

Banks are honest and follow Nacha. Fraud engines are out of scope: we target the authorization and settlement logic.

02 · Core insight & method

Three events, three institutions, one gap

Authorization, posting, and settlement occur at different institutions at different times. If credit becomes spendable before the debit passes the receiving bank's access control, one ledger shows money that never left the other. We call this Credit-Without-Debit.

How we found it

We converted the Nacha rules into a formal model and asked a model checker to break three invariants. Click a stage.

Three invariants every transfer should keep

Φ₁
Continuous Authorization
A settled entry stays authorized until settlement.
EntrySettled(E,o,r) ⇒ Authorized(E,o,r) ∧ ¬Revoked(E,o,r)
Φ₂
Zero-Sum
In every cycle, credits equal debits.
CycleComplete(c) ⇒ ⋀ₖ CountCredits(c,k) = CountDebits(c,k)
Φ₃
Settlement Finality
Nothing is released before the return window closes.
ResourceAvailable(r,E) ⇒ ReturnWindowClosed(E)

03 · Key results

Every invariant broke, and every attack worked on live accounts

We replayed each counterexample with ordinary customer actions on real accounts at 2 of the 4 largest U.S. ACH banks, 3 major card issuers, and 3 leading payment providers.

What to fix

Continuous authorization Φ₁
Check debit eligibility again at posting and before settlement.
Isolate provisional credit Φ₃
Do not let in-flight funds be spent or restore a credit line.
Zero-sum across institutions Φ₂
Lock the credit until the matching debit commits.
Symmetric account linking Φ₂
Verify debits, not only credits. A failed reclaim fails verification.
