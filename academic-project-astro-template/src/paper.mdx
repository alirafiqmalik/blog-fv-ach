<section id="overview">

## Why this matters

Most people assume a bank transfer is done the moment the money shows up. It is not. Your bank often lets you spend the transfer days before the other bank has checked whether the debit is even allowed.

That fronted cash is a **provisional credit**. It looks like a real balance. You can spend it, send it, or pay a bill with it. Banking payment systems move $100 trillion a year worldwide, and more than $30 trillion of that in the United States.

Settlement is the return window closing without a return arriving.

<HighlightedSection>

A bank transfer takes days to settle, and the bank that starts it often lets you spend the money long before the other bank has checked whether the debit is even allowed.

We call that gap the **Phantom Funds Exploit**: three ways to spend, collect, or restore money that was never actually sent.

We fed the 728-page Nacha rulebook into a simulator, proved the gap is written into the rules, and then triggered it on real accounts at real institutions.

We disclosed in April 2025; by January 2026 none of the three variations still worked. Consumers were never at risk.

</HighlightedSection>

<section id="attacks">

## What we found

Attackers do not break the banks. They use the days between “the money appears” and “the other bank is asked.”

How the gap works:

1. You ask your bank to pull money. It checks that the numbers look valid. It does not call the other bank. Those numbers are printed on the front of every cheque.
2. Your bank posts a provisional credit. The money appears. You can spend it.
3. For one to five days, the other bank has not been asked whether the debit is allowed.
4. If the account is locked, the debit is refused (`R16`) and the credit is clawed back — after it could have been spent. There is no rate limit on trying again.
5. That window is the exploit. Same gap, three disguises.

<section id="impact">

## What we tested

We triggered the exploit on accounts we owned, using ordinary consumer apps. Two of the largest U.S. banks. Three credit-card issuers. Three payment apps.

**No third-party account was affected at any point.** Consumers were never at risk. We disclosed in April 2025; by January 2026 none of the three variations still worked.
