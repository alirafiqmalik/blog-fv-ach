<section id="overview">

## Why This Matters

People assume bank transfers are instant and permanent. A card charge can be
disputed. A bank transfer is supposed to move real money, once, and for good.

The reality is slower and stranger. A US bank transfer is a five-day process.
Banks often front the money as provisional credit before anyone checks whether
the other account can pay.

The money shows up. You can spend it. Nothing has settled.

More than $30 trillion moves this way in the United States alone. Banking
payment systems worldwide move over $100 trillion every year.

<StakesBar />

A transfer stays pending for one to three banking days. If the debit fails, the
other bank sends back a return code — R16 if the account is blocked — and your
bank takes the credit back.

Settlement is the return window closing without a return arriving. All three
variations of the Phantom Funds Exploit are built out of that delay.

## What We Found

### {PHANTOM_FUNDS.name}

Attackers exploit that multi-day settlement gap. Money appears — and can be
spent — before the debit is verified.

The sending bank is only asking whether the routing number looks valid. It also
needs to ask whether the debit is actually allowed.

<section id="method">

## Formal Modeling of Nacha

We fed the 728-page Nacha rulebook into a mathematical simulator. The book lists
25 payment types, 87 return codes, and 14 change notices.

Modeling all of it is not possible. We scoped the simulator to ordinary consumer
transfers: paying a card, linking an account, sending money through an app.

<RulesFacts />

Then we hit a different problem. The rules do not say what the system should do.

<HighlightedSection>

> An Originator of a debit WEB Entry must establish and implement **commercially
> reasonable methods of authentication** to verify the identity of the Receiver
> of the debit WEB Entry.

<p class="text-sm text-[var(--text-muted)]">Nacha Operating Rules, Article 2, Subsection 2.5.17.5</p>

</HighlightedSection>

The rules never define "commercially reasonable". They never define "promptly".
They define "Banking Day" twice, in ways that disagree.

When the rulebook permits several behaviours, we modelled all of them. If a bank
is allowed to do it, the simulator gets to try it.

The gaps are structural blind spots written into the rules, not isolated bugs at
one bank.

</section>

<section id="evidence">

## What We Tested

We did not have bank logs. We had dashboards.

Every observation on this page is something a customer can see: a balance, a
timestamp, a status, a return code. Two of the largest U.S. banks, three
credit-card issuers, and three payment apps. All accounts our own — one active,
one dormant.

We safely triggered the exploit across those institutions, using only accounts
we owned.

**No third-party account was affected at any point.**

</section>

<section id="impact">

## Real-World Impact

We disclosed in April 2025. By January 2026 none of the three variations still
worked. Consumers were never at risk.

<DisclosureStrip />

The three specific attacks are closed. The gap they used is not: banks still
cannot look up another institution's account status, debit capability, or
settlement in real time. Closing that is a change to the rulebook, not a patch
at one bank.

</section>

trace are in the

Two things are not there. The Nacha Operating Rules are Nacha's copyright, so we

## Ethics

Every test ran against accounts owned by members of the research team. No
third-party account was affected at any point, and every charge was
self-contained. The institutions bearing the losses were told promptly, and we
disclosed through official channels before publishing.
