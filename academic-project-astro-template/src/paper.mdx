We formally verified the system that moves America's paychecks — and found three
ways to spend money that was never debited.

This is not a magic trick. No cryptography was broken. No bank was hacked. We
used ordinary consumer apps and accounts we owned. The system did exactly what
the rules allow.

## Bank transfers feel safer than cards

A card charge can be disputed. A bank transfer moves the money and it is gone —
permanently, between institutions, under 728 pages of rules.

So everyone assumes the obvious thing: you cannot be credited money nobody was
debited.

Nobody routinely verifies that assumption. We did.

<section id="how-ach-works">

<p class="act-kicker">Act II — The gap</p>

## The five-day life of a transfer

An ACH transfer is not one event. It is five.

**You** ask your bank to pull money from another account. Your bank — the
**ODFI** — checks the routing number, the account number, and the format. It
does not ask the other bank anything.

The entry waits in a **batch**. An **ACH Operator** routes it. The other bank —
the **RDFI** — is the first party in the chain to check whether that account
actually permits the debit.

That check happens days after your bank already decided.

In between, your bank does something helpful and dangerous: it posts a
**provisional credit**. The money shows up. You can spend it. Nothing has
settled.

<Wide>
  <AchPipeline client:visible />
</Wide>

A transfer stays pending for one to three banking days. If the debit fails, the
RDFI sends back a **return code** — `R16` if the account is blocked for debits —
and your bank claws the credit back.

Settlement is not an event either. **Settlement is the return window closing
without a return arriving.** Hold onto that sentence. All three attacks are
built out of it.

### Three ways to link an account

Before any of this, you have to prove the account is yours. The rules do not say
how.

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**Manual link.** Type in the routing and account numbers. They are printed on
the front of every cheque.

**Web login.** Authenticate at the external bank's own portal, often through an
aggregator such as Plaid.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Micro-deposits.** Two credits under a dollar land in the account; you report
the amounts back to prove you own it.

Payment providers usually combine the first and the third. That combination is
where A2 lives.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

<section id="method">

## From 728 pages of rules to a machine

The Nacha Operating Rules are 728 pages. 25 SEC codes. 87 return codes. 14
change notification codes.

You cannot model that. Modeling all of it is undecidable.

So we scoped it: domestic, standard, consumer ACH — paying a credit-card bill,
linking an account, sending money through a payment app. That is where the
volume is, and where the consumer fraud is.

<RulesFunnel client:visible />

Then we hit a different problem. The rules do not say what the system should do.

<HighlightedSection>

> An Originator of a debit WEB Entry must establish and implement **commercially
> reasonable methods of authentication** to verify the identity of the Receiver
> of the debit WEB Entry.

<p class="text-sm text-[var(--text-muted)]">Nacha Operating Rules, Article 2, Subsection 2.5.17.5</p>

</HighlightedSection>

The rules never define "commercially reasonable". They never define "promptly".
They define "Banking Day" twice, in ways that disagree. And in one place they
say a batch will be rejected for an error that another place says can never
cause a rejection.

When the specification permits several behaviours, we modelled all of them. If a
bank is allowed to do it, the model checker gets to try it.

### The model

Five communicating state machines — Originator, ODFI, ACH Operator, RDFI,
Receiver — over two channels. `C_info` carries the instructions. `C_$` moves the
money, later. Then an adversary, built only out of things ordinary customers can
do.

<Wide>
  <ModelGraph client:visible />
</Wide>

The state space stays finite by bounding it: each Originator initiates at most
two transactions, each batch holds at most three entries, and each run covers
three batch processing windows.

<section id="attacks">

<p class="act-kicker">Act III — The break</p>

## Three attacks

Each one has the same shape: something is made available at one institution
before another institution has been asked whether it is allowed. What differs is
*what* becomes available — funds, deposits, a credit line — and which invariant
that breaks.

The system is only asking: *is the routing number valid, and did this look like
a real transfer?* It also needs to ask: *is this debit actually allowed?*

<Wide>

| | A1: Authorization-as-Access-Control | A2: Micro-Deposit Verification | A3: Premature Posting-as-Settlement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Design rationale** | Provide immediate fund availability | Simplify account linking | Shorten credit-line recovery wait |
| **Implicit assumption** | Routing/account format ⇒ debit capability | Inbound credit ⇒ outbound debit | Internal posting ⇒ ACH settlement |
| **Exploit** | ODFI credit without RDFI debit | Credit without debit | Unbounded credit line |
| **Impact** | Access funds on authorization | Repeatedly accumulate micro-deposits | Spend on prematurely restored credit line |
| **Invariant broken** | Φ1 ✗ | Φ2 ✗ | Φ3 ✗ |

</Wide>

### A1 — Authorization-as-Access-Control

Your bank lets you link an account at another bank by typing in a routing number
and an account number. That is the whole check.

<Wide>
  <AttackScene attack="a1" client:visible />
</Wide>

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**Root cause.** The ODFI validates only routing and account number *format* when
initiating a debit, without checking whether the target account is locked or
debit-restricted. It then grants provisional credit immediately, assuming
settlement will succeed, even though the actual fund transfer takes up to five
days. Restriction status is discovered only when the RDFI rejects the debit.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Structural gap.** ACH provides **no real-time cross-institution account-status
query primitive**. Its absence forces ODFIs to commit liquidity at
routing-validation time and to learn restriction status only when a return code
arrives during settlement.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

<Figure>
  <img slot="figure" src={fig3.src} alt="Sequence diagram of the authorization-as-access-control vulnerability: the attacker's bank grants provisional credit on day 0–3, the exploitation window opens, and the victim's bank returns R16 on day 2–5." class="mx-auto max-w-full rounded-lg bg-[#f4f4f4] p-3" loading="lazy" />
  <span slot="caption">**Figure 3.** Authorization-as-access-control vulnerability in ACH debit processing. The exploitation window is the vertical bar on the left: it opens when the credit is granted and closes only when the return arrives.</span>
</Figure>

A1 is feasible against any account whose routing and account numbers are known.
When the targeted account is *not* debit-restricted, the money comes out of it —
with no additional verification anywhere in the chain.

### A2 — Micro-Deposit Verification

To link your bank account, an app sends two deposits under a dollar. You report
the amounts back. That proves you own the account — and then the app takes its
money back.

Except it cannot, if the account accepts credits and blocks debits. ACH accounts
support exactly that asymmetry.

<Wide>
  <AttackScene attack="a2" client:visible />
</Wide>

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**Root cause.** The flow assumes credit success implies debit capability, but
debit permission is enforced only at withdrawal, after the credits are already
posted. Providers further allow verification restarts **without rate limiting**,
so each reset issues new micro-deposits and recovers none of the old ones.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Structural gap.** ACH provides **no pre-flight debit-capability query
primitive**. Its absence forces providers to find out whether an account permits
debits only by submitting an actual debit and watching what comes back.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

<Figure>
  <img slot="figure" src={fig6.src} alt="Sequence diagram of the micro-deposit flaw: link and unlink repeat n times, each cycle posting ACH credits that succeed while the reclamation debits are returned with code R16." class="mx-auto max-w-full rounded-lg bg-[#f4f4f4] p-3" loading="lazy" />
  <span slot="caption">**Figure 6.** Asymmetric access-control flaw in micro-deposit verification. The `n repetitions` bracket at the top is the whole attack: nothing rate-limits it.</span>
</Figure>

### A3 — Premature Posting-as-Settlement

You pay your credit-card bill from your bank account. Two days later the card
says paid, balance zero, full limit restored.

The bank has not sent the money yet. It will take about five more days.

The card issuer is not waiting for the bank. It is waiting for its own
bookkeeping.

<Wide>
  <AttackScene attack="a3" client:visible />
</Wide>

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**Root cause.** The issuer restores the credit limit upon internal posting,
treating it as settlement confirmation despite having no guarantee the underlying
ACH debit will succeed. Because ACH failures surface only days later, the issuer
commits to a financial state before the funds are confirmed.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Structural gap.** ACH provides **no proactive settlement-status query
primitive**. Its absence forces issuers to learn settlement only
retrospectively — through the *absence* of a return before the window closes.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

There is a second, quieter consequence. The issuer reversed the payment but did
not charge interest — an APR of about 26% — for the days the restored credit was
in use, because its own ledger had said *paid* the whole time. A cardholder with
no intention of spending a cent can use this to avoid interest.

</section>

Eight institutions is a small sample. It is a small sample of a system that moves
$30 trillion a year in the United States alone, using workflows that are the same
everywhere because they are defined by the same rulebook. The three gaps the
model found — no account-status query, no debit-capability query, no
settlement-status query — are properties of ACH, not of any one bank's
implementation.

## Closing the gap

Five remedies. Three of them restore an invariant directly; the fourth closes the
linking asymmetry that A2 rides on; the fifth is the one that would prevent the
next three attacks rather than these three.

<InvariantCards variant="remedy" client:visible />

**Specification-level clarification.** Payment network standards must say
whether authorization is ongoing or a one-time event, whether provisional credits
are user-visible, and how settlement failures surface in user-visible state.
Underspecification is what allows protocol-compliant but insecure executions —
every attack on this page is permitted by the rules as written.

And here is the same five-day pipeline from the top of the page, with the credit
waiting for the check that was always supposed to come first:

<Wide>
  <AchPipeline mode="closed" client:visible />
</Wide>

A credit is only money if someone was actually debited. In this system, that was
optional. An expiration date is only useful if every part of the chain agrees on
what it means. The same is true of a bank transfer.

<section id="reference">

## The short version

<HighlightedSection>

An ACH transfer takes days to settle, and the bank that starts it makes the money
spendable long before the bank on the other end has checked whether the debit is
even allowed.

We modelled that workflow from the Nacha rules as five communicating state
machines, added an adversary that can only do things ordinary customers can do,
and asked a model checker whether three basic guarantees survive.

They do not: the checker returned three counterexamples, and all three reproduced
on real accounts at real institutions.

We disclosed in April 2025; by January 2026 none of the three reproduced.

</HighlightedSection>
