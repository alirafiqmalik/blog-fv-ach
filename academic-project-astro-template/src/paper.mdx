<section id="introduction">

## Introduction

In 2025 the top 50 U.S. institutions processed 30.7 billion ACH payments. 20 billion of those were at Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, and Citibank. Direct deposit, bill pay, mortgage servicing, and credit-card payments move on that network, and so do the bank links behind Zelle, PayPal, Venmo, and Cash App. Nacha writes the Operating Rules.

A card charge can be disputed. An ACH transfer, once settled, stays moved. Settlement is the return window closing without a return arriving, and that window lasts days. Before it closes, the originating bank (the ODFI) can post a provisional credit, money the customer can spend, while the receiving bank (the RDFI) has not checked the debit.

Participants expect the debit and the credit to happen together, and they expect an in-progress transfer not to change what someone can spend. The rules do not guarantee either. We asked whether bank-transfer systems enforce those guarantees when a payment crosses several institutions and settles in stages.

</section>

<section id="method">

## Brief methodology

The Nacha Operating Rules are 728 pages, with 25 SEC codes, 87 return codes, and 14 change notification codes. The number of participants is a parameter, so a model of the whole rulebook is undecidable. We aimed for soundness. If the checker reports a violation, it is a violation. We do not claim to have found every one.

The model covers domestic consumer ACH under the WEB and PPD codes, the entries a person starts on the internet. One obligation we had to interpret:

> An Originator of a debit WEB Entry must establish and implement **commercially reasonable methods of authentication** to verify the identity of the Receiver of the debit WEB Entry.
>
> Nacha Operating Rules, Article 2, Subsection 2.5.17.5

"Commercially reasonable" is not defined. Where the rules allow more than one behavior, the model includes each of them.

Five parties handle a transfer: the originator, the ODFI, an ACH operator, the RDFI, and the receiver. Instructions move on one channel. The money moves later, on another. The adversary can only do what a customer can do. Open an account, read a routing number, start a payment in an app, or place a debit block on an account they control. The banks follow the rules.

nuXmv's IC3/PDR engine checked three properties. Continuous authorization says a settled entry was authorized at submission and was not revoked before settlement. The zero-sum property says that, in a finished cycle, credits and debits match inside each amount class. Settlement finality says a balance or a credit line becomes usable only after the return window has closed. A run allows two transactions per originator, three entries in a batch, and three batch windows. A counterexample inside that bound is a witness.

</section>

<section id="attack">

## creditBLEED

Your bank lets you link another account by typing a routing number and an account number. That is the whole check. The ODFI accepts those numbers and posts a provisional credit. In our test, Bank A made $1,000 available three days after submission. Bank B had not been asked whether the debit was allowed. The account at Bank B was dormant and blocked for debits, and Bank B did not debit it.

<AttackScene attack="a1" client:visible />

Several days later Bank B returned R16, account blocked for debits, and Bank A reversed the credit. If the targeted account is not debit-restricted, the money comes out of it. Routing and account numbers are printed on a cheque.

We used accounts we owned, one active and one dormant, at two of the four banks named above. Those institutions asked not to be named. No third-party account was affected at any point.

The paper reports two further instances of the same shape, creditMINT and creditRESET. We disclosed all three on 16 April 2025. creditBLEED and creditRESET no longer reproduced by May 2025. creditMINT was still possible at one provider in December 2025, and that provider had closed it by January 2026.

The nuXmv model and the scripts that reproduce the counterexamples are at [doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22701199](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22701199).
