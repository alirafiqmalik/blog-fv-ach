<section id="overview">

<p class="act-kicker">Why this matters</p>

<Wide>
  <HeroScene client:load />
</Wide>

People assume a bank transfer is done the moment the money shows up. It is not. A typical transfer on the US bank network is a five-day process, and your bank often fronts the cash long before anyone checks the other account.

That fronted cash is called a **provisional credit**. It looks like a real balance. You can spend it, transfer it, or pay a bill with it.

The stakes are not small. Banking payment systems move $100 trillion a year worldwide. More than $30 trillion of that runs through the United States alone.

<ScaleCounters client:visible />

## The money shows up first

A card charge can be disputed. A bank transfer feels final — the money moves, and it is gone.

So everyone assumes the obvious thing: you cannot be credited money nobody was debited.

That assumption is the whole security model. We checked whether the system actually enforces it.

<Wide>
  <SplitLedger client:visible />
</Wide>

## Five days, not five seconds

An ACH transfer is not one event. It is a chain of five.

You ask your bank to pull money from another account. Your bank checks that the routing number and account number look valid. It does not call the other bank.

Then it does something helpful, and dangerous: it posts a provisional credit. The money appears. You can spend it. Nothing has settled.

<Wide>
  <AchPipeline client:visible />
</Wide>

If the other bank later refuses the debit, a return code comes back — `R16` if the account is locked — and your bank claws the credit back.

Settlement is not a moment of truth. **Settlement is the return window closing without a return arriving.** Hold onto that sentence. The Phantom Funds Exploit is built out of it.

</section>

<section id="attacks">

<p class="act-kicker">What we found</p>

## The Phantom Funds Exploit

Attackers do not break the banks. They use the multi-day gap.

While a transfer is still in flight, one institution has already made the money usable. The institution on the other end has not been asked whether the debit is even allowed.

We found three ways to ride that same gap. Same flaw. Three disguises.

<Wide>

| | Fake Authorization | Micro-Deposit Trap | Credit Line Mirage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What you see** | Money appears after you type two numbers | Tiny deposits land and never leave | The card says paid in two days |
| **The trick** | Link a locked account | An account that takes deposits but blocks withdrawals | Pay the card from a locked account |
| **The payoff** | Spend funds nobody was charged | Verification cents on a loop | Instantly restored credit limit |
| **Impact** | Provisional cash you can spend for days | Credits that cannot be reclaimed | Spend on a payment that never arrives |

</Wide>

### Fake Authorization

Your bank lets you link an account at another bank by typing in a routing number and an account number. That is the whole check. Those numbers are printed on the front of every cheque.

<Wide>
  <AttackScene attack="a1" client:visible />
</Wide>

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**How it works.** We linked a locked account we owned, then pulled $1,000 from it. Our bank showed the credit and made it spendable. The other bank had not been asked anything.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Why it works.** Banks cannot ask each other, in real time, whether an account is locked. Your bank commits the money first and finds out days later, when a return arrives.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

Do it again and you get another $1,000. And another. The locked account's balance never moves.

### The Micro-Deposit Trap

To link your bank account, an app sends two deposits under a dollar. You report the amounts back. That proves you own the account — and then the app tries to take its money back.

Except it cannot, if the account accepts deposits and blocks withdrawals. ACH accounts can be set up exactly that way.

<Wide>
  <AttackScene attack="a2" client:visible />
</Wide>

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**How it works.** The deposits land. The reclaim fails. The app lets you cancel and start over, as many times as you like, with no rate limit.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Why it works.** A successful deposit is not proof that a withdrawal will work. There is no way to ask in advance. Each restart sends new cents, and none of them come back.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

Three major payment apps. Every reclaim failed.

### The Credit Line Mirage

You pay your credit-card bill from your bank account. Two days later the card says paid, balance zero, full limit restored.

The bank has not sent the money yet. It will take about five more days. The card issuer is not waiting for the bank. It is waiting for its own bookkeeping.

<Wide>
  <AttackScene attack="a3" client:visible />
</Wide>

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**How it works.** We paid a card from an account locked for withdrawals. The issuer posted the payment, restored the limit, and we spent it. Then we did it again.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**Why it works.** The issuer treats its own posting as the real payment. There is no way to ask whether the bank transfer has actually settled. The bounce arrives about six days later.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

The issuer reversed the payment. It did not charge interest for the days the restored credit was in use, because its own ledger had said *paid* the whole time.

</section>

<section id="method">

<p class="act-kicker">The rulebook</p>

## We put Nacha into a simulator

The Nacha Operating Rules run the US bank-transfer network. They are 728 pages long, with 25 payment types and 87 ways a transfer can bounce.

You cannot check every page by hand. So we fed the consumer path — paying a card, linking an account, sending money through an app — into a mathematical simulator.

<RulesFunnel client:visible />

Then we hit a different problem. The rules often refuse to say what a bank must do.

<HighlightedSection>

> An Originator of a debit WEB Entry must establish and implement **commercially
> reasonable methods of authentication** to verify the identity of the Receiver
> of the debit WEB Entry.

<p class="text-sm text-[var(--text-muted)]">Nacha Operating Rules, Article 2, Subsection 2.5.17.5</p>

</HighlightedSection>

The rules never define "commercially reasonable". They never define "promptly". They define "Banking Day" twice, in ways that disagree.

When the rulebook allowed more than one behavior, we let the simulator try all of them. If a bank is allowed to do it, an attacker gets to try it too.

### Three things that should always be true

<InvariantCards client:visible />

The simulator found a way to break each one. These are not one-off bugs at a single bank. They are structural blind spots, written directly into the rules.

<TwoColumns>
  <div slot="left">

**What the attacker needs.** Real accounts, opened normally. Routing and account numbers — printed on the front of every cheque. Ordinary consumer apps. A debit block on their *own* account.

  </div>
  <div slot="right">

**What the attacker does not need.** A stolen password. A hacked bank. Broken cryptography. Every capability here is a feature the system already offers customers.

  </div>
</TwoColumns>

</section>

<p class="act-kicker">What we tested</p>

## This is not a thought experiment

We triggered the exploit on accounts we owned, using ordinary consumer apps. Two of the largest U.S. banks. Three credit-card issuers. Three payment apps.

Every observation on this page is something a customer can see: a balance, a timestamp, a status, a return code.

**No third-party account was affected at any point.** Consumers were never at risk. The institutions that advanced the money were the ones that lost it, and we told them.

<section id="reference">

## The short version

<HighlightedSection>

A bank transfer takes days to settle, and the bank that starts it often lets you spend the money long before the other bank has checked whether the debit is even allowed.

We call that gap the Phantom Funds Exploit: three ways to spend, collect, or restore money that was never actually sent.

We fed the 728-page Nacha rulebook into a simulator, proved the gap is written into the rules, and then triggered it on real accounts at real institutions.

We disclosed in April 2025; by January 2026 none of the three variations still worked. Consumers were never at risk.

</HighlightedSection>
