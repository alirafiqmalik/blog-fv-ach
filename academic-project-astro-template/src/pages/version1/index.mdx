<p className="act-kicker">Act I · Why ACH matters</p>

## The network under your salary

Your salary arrives by bank transfer. Your mortgage, your credit-card bill and your other bills leave the same way. When you link a bank account to Zelle, PayPal, Venmo or Cash App, the link underneath is the same kind of transfer.

In the United States those transfers run on the Automated Clearing House, the ACH network.

<ScaleCounters client:visible />

Bank transfers are supposed to be the safe kind of payment. A card charge can be disputed. A bank transfer moves the money for good: once it settles it cannot be reversed, and it gets there through several stages of clearing between banks, under strict operating rules.

So everyone assumes a few things without ever checking them:

1. If money is credited to one account, it was debited from another.
2. The two balances never disagree halfway through.
3. A transfer that has not finished does not change what you can spend.
4. Once a transfer looks complete, it stays complete.

None of these is guaranteed by the ACH network, and none is written into regulation.

### One transfer, step by step

An ACH transfer is not one event. It is a chain of them, across five parties, over several days. The figure from the top of the page again, this time walked.

<AchOverview mode="walk" />

In the meantime your bank has done something helpful and dangerous. It has posted a **provisional credit**. The money shows up. You can spend it. Nothing has settled.

A transfer stays pending for one to three banking days. If the debit fails, their bank sends back a return code, `R16` if the account is blocked for debits, and your bank claws the credit back.

Settlement is not an event either. Settlement is the return window closing without a return arriving.

<HighlightedSection>

> Our question, exactly as the paper asks it: *do bank-transfer payment systems actually enforce the security guarantees they are assumed to provide when transactions span multiple institutions and asynchronous settlement stages?*

</HighlightedSection>

<Disclosure summary="Why the question is hard: three things the standards leave open">

The Nacha Operating Rules describe how transactions are initiated and settled, but leave three security-critical questions underspecified:

- whether authorization is a one-time check or must hold continuously,
- whether provisional credits may be exposed before the corresponding debits succeed,
- how failures are handled when they occur days after a transaction appears complete.

These gaps are amplified by the structure of bank transfers, which unfold over multiple days and across independent institutions that enforce access control locally and at different times. Unlike card networks, which rely on issuer guarantees and chargebacks, bank transfers move funds permanently between institutions through multi-stage interbank clearing, so a mistake in the ordering of those stages is not something a customer can dispute away afterwards.

</Disclosure>

<p className="act-kicker">Act II · What we did</p>

## We let a machine try every move the rules allow

We wrote down the rules of the transfer as a model, and let a machine try every move the rules allow.

A formal model is a precise description of who can do what, and when. A model checker is a program that explores every sequence of moves the model permits and reports whether a property you care about can ever be broken. When it can, the checker hands back the exact sequence of moves that breaks it. That sequence is called a counterexample, and it is a recipe.

### Scope

The Nacha Operating Rules run to 728 pages: 25 SEC codes, 87 return codes, 14 change notification codes. Modelling all of it is not slow. It is undecidable.

So we scoped it to the transfers a person starts on the internet: paying a credit-card bill, linking an account, sending money through a payment app. That is where the volume is, and where the consumer fraud is.

### The model

Five parties, each modelled as a small machine that moves between states when it receives a message: you, your bank, the rails, their bank, them. Two channels connect them. One carries instructions. The other carries money, and it moves later.

Then we added an adversary who can do only what any customer can do.

<CapabilityList />

Nothing here is exotic. Every capability is a feature.

<Disclosure summary="The model, in the paper's terms: state machines, channels, time, and the bounds">

**Participant FSMs.** Communicating finite state machines for Originators (O), the ODFI (BO), the ACH Operator (A), the RDFI (BR) and Receivers (R). Each template is a tuple F = (S, s₀, V, Σ, δ): a finite set of states, an initial state, local state variables, a message alphabet, and guarded transitions δ : S × Guards → S × Actions. Transitions are labelled `guard/action`; guards are propositional formulas over local variables, received messages and environment variables, and every transition has a non-empty guard so state progression is deterministic. Environment variables are Boolean flags the model checker sets non-deterministically to explore alternative paths.

**Channels.** Only the message fields that matter for authorization and settlement are modelled: account numbers, routing numbers, transaction codes, amounts. Two channels: `C_info` carries payment instructions, acknowledgments and settlement data between participants; `C_$` executes fund transfers during settlement.

**Time.** Clock ticks would explode the state space, so a timing window is a pair of Booleans, `event_started` and `event_expired`; an ongoing settlement window is `settlement_started ∧ ¬settlement_expired`. The rule "an ACH credit initiated Wednesday by 5:00 p.m. for Friday settlement must be available by 9:00 a.m. Friday" becomes

```
complete := achCredit(Oi, Rj, e) ∧ transfer_started(e)
          ∧ settlement_started(e) ∧ ¬settlement_expired(e)
          ⟹ FundsAvailable(Rj, e)
```

**Batches.** Each transaction carries its own `payment_state` over `{ not_submitted, submitted, batched, pending_settlement, settled, rejected, returned, reversed }`. Batch transmission is one synchronized transition fired by `batch_expired`: every `submitted` entry becomes `batched` atomically.

**Composition and bounds.** M = (E, O¹…O^nO, BO, A, BR, R¹…R^nR, C_info, C_$), where E controls global timing. The state space stays finite because each Originator initiates at most two transactions, each batch holds at most three entries, and three batch processing windows are modelled per run. M^adv extends M by instrumenting Originators and Receivers with non-deterministic transitions for the four capabilities above, so the checker explores every attack strategy the specification permits.

</Disclosure>

### The rules do not say

Then we hit a different problem. The rules do not say what the system should do.

<HighlightedSection>

> "An Originator of a debit WEB Entry must establish and implement **commercially reasonable methods of authentication** to verify the identity of the Receiver of the debit WEB Entry."
>
> Nacha Operating Rules, Article 2, Subsection 2.5.17.5

</HighlightedSection>

The rules never define "commercially reasonable". They never define "promptly". They define "Banking Day" twice, in ways that disagree. And in one place they say a batch will be rejected for an error that another place says can never cause a rejection.

Wherever the rules allow more than one behaviour, we modelled all of them. If a bank is allowed to do it, the checker gets to try it.

### Three things that should always be true

<InvariantCards variant="intro" client:visible />

The one in the title is the second. A credit must be matched by a debit.

<Disclosure summary="The three invariants as the checker sees them">

**Φ1, Continuous Authorization.** Every settled entry must have been submitted while authorized and must not have been revoked between submission and settlement:

```
∀ o, r, E . EntrySettled(E, o, r) ⟹ Authorized(E, o, r) ∧ ¬Revoked(E, o, r)
```

`EntrySettled` is an ODFI-local commitment event: it holds as soon as the ODFI posts a provisional credit, and stays true even if the ACH debit is later returned. An RDFI-side debit restriction that blocks settlement yields counterexamples in the same predicate pattern as a mid-window revocation; the abstraction is conservative and yields no false positives.

**Φ2, Zero-Sum.** For any complete cycle, credits and debits balance within each amount class:

```
∀ c . CycleComplete(c) ⟹ ⋀_{k ∈ K} CountCredits(c, k) = CountDebits(c, k)
```

with K = \{ micro, regular \}. A cycle is complete when every entry in it has reached a terminal state: settled, returned or reversed.

**Φ3, Settlement Finality.** A resource that depends on an entry may be released only after that entry's return window has closed:

```
∀ r, E . ResourceAvailable(r, E) ⟹ ReturnWindowClosed(E)
```

Settlement is operationally "the return window closed without an intervening return", so requiring window closure subsumes settlement as the irrevocability condition.

</Disclosure>

### How the checker finds a violation

We gave the model to nuXmv, a model checker. For each property it does one of two things: it proves the property holds on every run the model allows, or it hands back a run that breaks it.

It handed back three.

A counterexample is a claim about a model. So for each one we set up real accounts to match its starting conditions, ran the steps through ordinary banking apps, and watched the dashboards: two checking accounts at two major U.S. banks, three credit cards, three payment-provider accounts. All ours.

<Disclosure summary="Verification and validation setup: nuXmv, IC3/PDR, and black-box observation">

**Composition.** The five participant FSMs are composable nuXmv modules communicating over two shared single-slot buses, `c_info_channel` and `c_dollar_channel`, implementing C_info and C_$. The top-level composition instantiates two adversarial Originators, two adversarial Receivers, one ODFI, one ACH Operator and one RDFI, plus the environment controller E.

**Checking.** Each property is a single `INVARSPEC` safety predicate over the state space of M^adv. nuXmv's `INVARSPEC` engine applies IC3/PDR backward reachability, which proves or refutes the invariant over all reachable states of the bounded model. M^adv was first validated against the Nacha Operating Rules through a counterexample-driven debugging loop, correcting the model whenever a trace exhibited specification-inconsistent behaviour, until every trace conformed.

**Validation.** Live consumer accounts: two checking accounts at two major U.S. banks (one active, one dormant), three credit-card accounts, and three accounts with third-party payment providers. Accounts were configured to match each counterexample's preconditions, the attack sequences were run manually through the banking portals, and the observable state changes were recorded: balances, timestamps, status transitions, fund availability, ACH return timing and final transaction states. There is no access to bank logs or ACH trace files, so validation is black-box, from user-visible artifacts in online banking dashboards.

The complete model source, property files and scripts to reproduce all counterexamples are at [doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22701199](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22701199).

</Disclosure>

## The credit posts before the other bank is asked

Bank A lets you link an account at Bank B by typing in a routing number and an account number. That is the whole check.

<AttackScene attack="a1" client:visible />

Root cause: Bank A checked that the routing number and the account number were well formed, then committed the money. It learned that the account was blocked only when the return code arrived, days later.

Structural gap: ACH has no way for one bank to ask another, in real time, whether an account can be debited. So the first bank commits at routing-validation time and finds out at return time.

Property broken: permission at the start was treated as permission all the way through.

<CauseAndGap attack="a1" />

We used our own dormant account. The same steps work against any account whose routing and account numbers are known, and if that account is not blocked for debits, the money comes out of it. Routing and account numbers are printed on the front of every cheque.

<Disclosure summary="The counterexample π₁, and the paper's sequence diagram">

An Originator submits an entry E targeting a debit-restricted Receiver account. The ODFI accepts the submission after `CheckRoutingValidity(routing_number) = VALID`; the entry advances to `AUTHORIZED`; the ODFI grants provisional credit; and several days later the RDFI returns R16 (Account Frozen/Blocked) and the entry transitions to `RETURNED`. `EntrySettled(E, o, r)` holds at the moment the ODFI posts the provisional credit, while `Authorized(E, o, r)` does not hold at the RDFI.

A1 is assigned to Φ1 because the exploitable defect is an authorization-architecture failure: the ODFI commits liquidity on the basis of routing-number validity alone, treating a single upfront check as continuous authorization through settlement. The provisional credit is a consequence of that failure rather than its cause.

### Two more of the same shape

<AttackCard attack="a2">

To link your bank account, an app sends two deposits under a dollar, and you report the amounts back. Then the app takes its money back. Except it cannot, if the account accepts credits and blocks debits, which ACH accounts can be configured to do.

The deposits land, the reclaim fails, and the app lets you cancel verification and start again, with no rate limit. Each restart sends new money. None of it comes back. **Three major payment providers; every reclaim failed.**

<AttackCard attack="a3">

You pay your credit-card bill from a bank account. Within two business days the card says paid, balance zero, full limit restored. The bank has not sent the money yet; that takes about five more days.

We paid three cards in full from an account blocked for debits. Each issuer posted the payment within two business days and restored the full credit limit while the ACH debit was still unsettled, which allowed immediate new spending. Schedule several payments and the available credit inflates several times over. **After about six business days the debit came back**, and no interest was charged for the days in between, because the ledger had said paid the whole time.

<section id="act4">

<p className="act-kicker">Act IV · Impact and disclosure</p>

## Reported, acknowledged, and closed

We reported all three to every affected institution on 16 April 2025, with reproduction steps, screenshots and technical reports.

<DisclosureList />

### Who lost money

In every experiment the loss fell on an institution, not a customer: the originating bank advanced funds it never collected, the payment provider issued deposits it could not reclaim, the card issuer restored a credit line against a payment that never arrived. Every account was ours, and no third-party account was affected at any point. The institutions bearing the losses were informed promptly.

One caveat belongs here rather than in a footnote. creditBLEED works against any account whose routing and account numbers are known, and when that account is not blocked for debits, the money leaves it. That is why it was reported first and closed first.

### The fix

The three properties from Act II come back, each with the remedy that restores it.

<InvariantCards variant="remedy" client:visible />

<SpecificationRemedy />

Three of those are a specification change, not a patch at one bank. The instances are fixed. The gaps are not.
